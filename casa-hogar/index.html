What Is a Casa Hogar?
A Children's Home in Chiapas

In Mexico, a children's home is called a casa hogar. This is one of them: Casa Hogar Alegre, where ninety-one children have somewhere to sleep, someone to call in the night, and a school to walk to in the morning. It has been open since 1993.

The word

Casa hogar or orphanage? Why Mexico uses a different word

The words translate as “home house,” and Mexicans use them instead of orfanato — orphanage — on purpose. An orphanage is where children are kept. A casa hogar is meant to be where they live.

The difference is not decoration. Most children in a casa hogar are not orphans: they have living relatives who cannot care for them, or must not. They arrive by order of a family court, often with a brother or sister, often with nothing. What they need is not custody. It is a household.

That is the standard Casa Hogar Alegre has been held to for thirty-three years.

The home

Casa Hogar Alegre: an orphanage in Tuxtla Gutiérrez, Chiapas

Chiapas is Mexico's southernmost state, on the Guatemalan border, and its poorest. Tuxtla Gutiérrez is the capital.

Elizabeth Castelazo — everyone calls her Mama Liz — opened the home in 1993 with her two children, Ángel and Merari. All three are still there.

Eighteen

Why children can stay past eighteen at this casa hogar

The youngest child here is one year old. The oldest is twenty-eight. That range is the thing that tells you most about how this home works.

Legally, the home's responsibility ends when a child turns eighteen. In practice, they can stay. A young person who wants to keep studying — trade school, university — stays, as long as their conduct is good. So does someone who has just started a first job and is saving to move out.

Ingrid is in her eighth semester of nursing school and still lives here.

Most institutions release young people at eighteen, because that is where their obligation ends. This one treats eighteen as the year someone starts deciding what to do — not the year they are asked to leave.

Every day

School, meals and care inside the casa hogar

Children do not leave the property to go to school. Casa Hogar Alegre runs its own instruction on campus, from maternal through high school:

  • Core academics — maths, science, reading
  • English as a second language
  • Music and cultural heritage
  • Vocational and technical training
  • Bible study

Trade school and university are off campus, and young people who go on to either keep their place at the home while they study.

Alongside the classrooms: three meals a day and a snack during school hours, medical care, on-site psychological support, and sport.

Funding

How this orphanage in Mexico is funded

Casa Hogar Alegre is privately funded. Food, the home's own school, medical care, the adults who raise the children and the buildings themselves are paid for by individual donors, by the groups who come to visit, and by some twenty partner organizations. That is how it has worked for thirty-three years.

There is no endowment and no guaranteed budget. What arrives in a month is what the home has to work with — and a short month is not an abstraction. It means fewer outings, less sport, less protein at the table, and school materials and maintenance that wait.

That is what a monthly sponsorship changes: it makes part of the year predictable.

Groups

Bringing a mission group to Casa Hogar Alegre

Casa Hogar Alegre hosts groups from churches and schools. It does not organize trips. If your group is already planning to come to Chiapas, the home will help you arrange your time here — what to work on, where to stay, how the days run.

Groups paint, build, run activities, and sit with the children. There is no minimum age: families have come with babies, and schools bring high-school students with teachers.

Everyone flies into Tuxtla Gutiérrez (TGZ) — from California, crossing to Tijuana through the CBX bridge for the direct TIJ–TGZ flight; from elsewhere, connecting through Mexico City or Cancún.

Most sponsors who have been here started as someone on a trip.
